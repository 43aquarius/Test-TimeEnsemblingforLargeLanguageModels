The sensitivity analysis in Table~\ref{tab:models} addresses the residual exclusions from two directions. Under a lenient aggregation rule that admits any question with at least four parsed votes per source, the ordering of conditions is preserved for every model: Agnes3.0-Flash moves from 78.8\% greedy to 88.1\% under SC-6 and 92.3\% under PE-6 on the extended set, and the flagship accuracies shift by at most one question. \subsection{RQ1: Accuracy under budget-matched ensembling}
\label{sec:rq1}

Table~\ref{tab:main} reports accuracy for the five aggregation conditions together with the two single-call baselines, and Figure~\ref{fig:conditions} visualizes the four models with at least twenty complete questions. Because the ensembles spend stochastic calls, the single-sample expectation S1 is the primary $k{=}1$ baseline, and the $\Delta$ columns of Table~\ref{tab:main} express both ensemble gains relative to it. Three patterns dominate. First, the two Qwen flagships are at or near ceiling on five of six benchmarks, and for them the pure ensembles reproduce both baselines exactly: SC-6, PE-6, and COMB-12 all return 93.3\% for Qwen3.8-Max and 93.0\% for Qwen3.8-Omni, identical to greedy and S1. The aggregation strategies are not failing; they are vacuous, because the answer distribution they aggregate contains a single value, a point that Section~\ref{sec:rq4} quantifies. Second, measured against S1, neither diversity source buys a systematic gain: pooled over the four well-covered models (112 model-question pairs on 28 common questions), SC-6 gains $+0.9$ points and PE-6 loses $0.9$ points, with the combined ensemble exactly matching S1; the largest per-model gain over S1 is $+1.5$ points (Agnes3.0-Flash, both sources). Third, the gains that do exist are concentrated where the modal answer is wrong: PE-6 lifts GPT-6-Luna by $+10.0$ points over S1 and Gemini3.8-Flash by $+5.6$, while SC-6 \emph{loses} $3.3$ and $2.8$ points on the same two models. The sign of the gain, not its size, is what separates the models, which is the plurality-vote signature of Section~\ref{sec:condorcet}.

A separate result concerns the greedy reference itself: greedy decoding at $\tau = 0$ is not an unbiased stand-in for a single stochastic draw. Pooled over the same 112 pairs, S1 reaches 96.4\% against 92.0\% for greedy, a $+4.5$ point gap whose question-level cluster-bootstrap interval, $[+0.9, +8.8]$, excludes zero. The gap is model dependent in both direction and mechanism. For the two Agnes models, greedy sits $10.6$ and $4.2$ points below S1; auditing the discordant questions shows one clear truncation artifact (a greedy chain-of-thought that hit the token cap, after which the parser extracted an intermediate number), two cases of mode locking where the greedy answer differed from every one of the six sampled answers, and remaining cases where greedy picks a minority answer. For GPT-6-Luna and Gemini3.8-Flash the sign reverses ($-3.3$ and $-5.6$ points), and for these models the greedy answer always coincided with the modal sampled answer on our data. Neither baseline subsumes the other, which is why Table~\ref{tab:main} reports both; but because the ensembles aggregate stochastic calls, S1 is the fair comparison, and gains measured against greedy conflate the ensembling effect with the greedy-versus-sampled gap. Earlier releases of this study reported the larger greedy-referenced gains ($+12.1$ points for Agnes3.0-Flash under SC-6); under the S1 baseline the same cell is $+1.5$ points.

Two further observations complete the accuracy picture. The hybrid allocation is a hedge rather than a dominant strategy: it matches the better pure strategy when one source fails, as for GPT-6-Luna where HB-6 and PE-6 both reach 86.7\% while SC-6 falls to 73.3\%, but it never exceeds the better pure strategy on any model, and it trails it by exactly one question on Qwen3.8-Max (92.2 versus 93.3), Qwen3.8-Omni (91.5 versus 93.0), and Agnes2.5-Flash (92.5 versus 95.0). The twelve-vote combined ensemble likewise does not dominate the six-vote conditions: pooled it exactly matches S1 at 96.4\% against 97.3\% for SC-6, because for three of the four pooled models the paraphrase votes it adds are individually no better than a single canonical draw and dilute the majority. Doubling the budget therefore buys no guarantee; the quality of the marginal vote, not the count, governs the outcome.

On the central question, whether one diversity source buys more accuracy per call than the other, we detect no difference against either baseline. Against S1, the pooled SC-6 advantage over PE-6 is $1.8$ points, the question-level cluster-bootstrap interval is $[0.0, +3.6]$, and the per-model intervals for the gain over S1 all include zero (Table~\ref{tab:tests}); no per-model McNemar test on the SC-6-versus-PE-6 discordances approaches significance (all $p \ge 0.5$), and the smallest McNemar $p$ anywhere in the table is $0.125$ (SC-6 versus greedy on Agnes3.0-Flash), which does not survive any multiple-comparison correction. We emphasize what this does and does not establish. The TOST procedure at equivalence bounds of $\pm 3$ points does not conclude equivalence for the pooled difference, and the bound itself is coarse at these sample sizes: $\pm 3$ points is 2.7 questions at $n = 90$ and fewer than one question on the smallest sets, so a single flipped question can move the verdict. At these sample sizes the study is powered to detect only large differences; a genuine advantage of either source of up to several points could go unnoticed. The two strategies are therefore not interchangeable in principle, and for two reasons the data show they are not interchangeable in practice either: they disagree on individual questions at measurable rates (Section~\ref{sec:rq2}), and their agreement signals catch different errors (Section~\ref{sec:rq3}).

The comparison is also matched in tokens, not only in calls. Averaged per call, canonical-sample and paraphrase calls cost within one to five percent of each other for every model (for example 223 versus 219 tokens for Qwen3.8-Max and 505 versus 528 for GPT-6-Luna), because validated paraphrases change the surface form but not the length of the task: the mean character-length ratio between paraphrase and original stems is 1.06 for the Qwen models and 1.13 to 1.14 for the API-served models. Budgets matched in calls are therefore approximately matched in cost for this suite.

\subsection{RQ2: Paraphrase fragility}
\label{sec:rq2}

Fragility, the fraction of questions where paraphrasing alone flips the majority answer, is negligible in the degenerate regime and substantial in the high-entropy regime. Qwen3.8-Max shows fragility on 1.1\% of questions and Qwen3.8-Omni on 4.2\%, concentrated almost entirely in LogiQA, where the respective per-dataset fragility rates are 6.7\% and 21.4\%. The models with high answer entropy on logical reasoning show more: GPT-6-Luna flips on 13.3\% of its questions overall and on 40\% of its LogiQA questions, and Gemini3.8-Flash on 8.3\% overall and 33.3\% of LogiQA. Agnes3.0-Flash, whose gains come with fully consistent votes across both sources, shows zero fragility.

Stratifying by paraphrase locus, which the design fixes in advance, shows that flips are entirely a stem-locus phenomenon. Across all six models, no question ever flips under instruction paraphrasing (zero of 52 pooled instruction-locus pairs), while 3.3\% of pooled stem-locus pairs flip, rising to 28.6\% and 20\% for GPT-6-Luna and Gemini3.8-Flash respectively. The two loci differ by construction in how much of the prompt they vary, so the asymmetry should be read as a property of the perturbation family rather than of the models: instruction rewordings touch only the answer-format scaffolding, while stem rewordings touch the problem content itself. For evaluation practice, the practical consequence is that a single-prompt score on a hard benchmark understates the instability of the measured system by an amount that stem fragility makes visible, and that instruction-level prompt ensembling, as sometimes used to stabilize benchmark scores, probes a much weaker axis of variation than stem-level paraphrasing.

Fragility is not random noise; it tracks the same model-task cells that carry answer entropy and ensembling gains, which supports reading it as an observable symptom of unstable knowledge rather than as a formatting artifact. It is also not an artifact of loose paraphrases: recomputing the statistic with only the paraphrases whose fresh embedding similarity exceeds the stricter 0.80 threshold, requiring at least four such paraphrases per question, leaves the rates essentially unchanged or higher for every model (GPT-6-Luna 33.3\% on six qualifying questions versus 13.3\% overall; Gemini3.8-Flash 25.0\% versus 8.3\%; Qwen3.8-Omni 4.8\% versus 4.2\%), so if anything the tighter paraphrases flip more. Section~\ref{sec:greedypara} returns to fragility with a decoding-noise-free variant computed from greedy paraphrase decodes.

\subsection{RQ3: Consistency as an error signal}
\label{sec:rq3}

Table~\ref{tab:uncert} evaluates agreement scores as unsupervised detectors of ensemble errors, and Figure~\ref{fig:auroc} visualizes AUROC. Models with fewer than five ensemble errors are marked with a dagger and reported descriptively only; at one to three errors, AUROC values are dominated by individual questions and their confidence intervals would span most of the unit interval. The pattern follows the regime structure. For the Qwen flagships, where almost every question is degenerate, sample agreement carries almost no signal (AUROC 0.58 for Qwen3.8-Max, 95\% CI $[0.50, 0.78]$) because the score is constant at one for nearly all questions, right or wrong; the degenerate stratum contributes errors that are invisible to any agreement-based detector by construction, and five of Qwen3.8-Max's six errors are missed by both signals. Qwen3.8-Omni retains moderate signal (0.79). In the models with genuine entropy the scores become informative and the stronger source differs across models: for Agnes3.0-Flash, sample agreement is highly discriminative (0.96, CI $[0.89, 1.00]$) while paraphrase agreement is weak (0.60), whereas for GPT-6-Luna paraphrase agreement is the stronger signal (0.92 against 0.58) and for Gemini3.8-Flash both signals separate its single error perfectly. Agnes2.5-Flash, whose answers are mostly degenerate, shows a reversed sample-agreement ranking that we read as noise at its two-error rate.

The risk-coverage analysis in Table~\ref{tab:uncert} quantifies the selective-prediction value of each signal, with ties in the confidence score resolved by uniformly random tie-breaking (Section~\ref{sec:method}), so each AURC is directly comparable with the model's overall error rate, which is the AURC of a random ranking. Sample agreement attains an AURC of 0.031 for Qwen3.8-Omni against an error rate of 7.0\%, and 0.009 for Agnes3.0-Flash against 9.1\%; paraphrase agreement attains 0.032 and 0.073 on the same models, so the AURC ordering tracks the AUROC ordering rather than the earlier fixed-order artifact, which had overstated Qwen3.8-Omni's ranking quality and placed Agnes3.0-Flash's paraphrase signal above its error rate. For Qwen3.8-Max, whose signals are uninformative, AURC stays at 0.057 against a 6.7\% error rate, close to the random baseline as expected. Pooled over the four well-covered models, sample agreement reaches an AUROC of 0.74 (cluster-bootstrap 95\% CI $[0.71, 0.75]$) against 0.60 $[0.46, 0.73]$ for paraphrase agreement, an interval that overlaps chance: pooled across models with opposite signal strengths, the paraphrase signal averages out.

The two agreement scores therefore measure different failure modes, but the evidence for complementarity is about coverage, not about a superior combined detector. Among the ensemble errors of the models with informative signals, the flagged sets overlap only partially: for Agnes3.0-Flash, sample agreement flags all three errors while paraphrase agreement flags one, two of them only through sample agreement; for GPT-6-Luna, paraphrase agreement flags both errors and sample agreement only one; for Qwen3.8-Omni the two signals flag the same three of five errors. Accordingly, the combined Both score exceeds the best single signal only for Gemini3.8-Flash, ties it for Agnes3.0-Flash (0.92 against 0.96 for sample agreement alone, i.e., slightly below), and falls between the two singles elsewhere; pooled it reaches 0.73 against 0.74 for sample agreement alone. A deployment that can afford both probes obtains partially disjoint evidence, and neither signal subsumes the other, but summing the two disagreement scores is not the way to combine them; a learned or calibrated combination is a natural next step. We also note the structural caveat of Section~\ref{sec:method}: on questions where all twelve votes coincide, the reference answer equals that unanimous vote by construction, so detectors that read unanimity are correct by definition on the degenerate stratum; the informative content of Table~\ref{tab:uncert} lies in the non-degenerate stratum, which the flagged-error analysis above reflects.

\subsection{RQ4: The regime structure}
\label{sec:rq4}

Table~\ref{tab:regime} reports per model and dataset the greedy accuracy, the single-sample expectation, the combined-ensemble accuracy, the mean modal vote share, and the degeneracy rate, and Figure~\ref{fig:regime} plots the ensemble gain over S1 against the dissent share (one minus the modal share) for every model-dataset cell. The scatter shows the dependency that anchors the whole study, in a sharper form than the entropy-based reading of our first release. Disagreement predicts the fragility of the greedy reference rather than the value of ensembling: across the pooled (model, question) pairs of the four well-covered models, the Spearman rank correlation between the dissent share and the gap $\mathrm{S1} - \mathrm{greedy}$ is $-0.80$, meaning that greedy decoding departs furthest from the sampled consensus exactly where the votes disagree, while the correlation between dissent and the COMB-12 gain over S1 is only $-0.17$: relative to a single stochastic draw, ensembling neither gains nor loses much on average, it only converts an uncertain single draw into the modal answer. Because the share and the combined ensemble share the same underlying votes, we recompute the correlations with strictly disjoint votes: the sample-side share against the PE-6 gain over S1 gives $+0.17$, and the paraphrase-side share against the SC-6 gain over S1 gives $-0.49$, both weak, confirming that the strong dependency sits in the greedy reference, not in the ensemble gain. Stratified by modal share, the pooled pairs split 104 unanimous, five with a strong majority (share at least two thirds), and three with a weak or no majority. In the unanimous stratum every condition sits at 98.1\%. In the five strong-majority pairs, S1 is 76.7\% and the six-vote ensembles 80.0\%. In the three weak-majority pairs, S1 is 72.2\%, SC-6 reaches 100\%, PE-6 falls to 33.3\%, and the combined ensemble 66.7\%; with three pairs these numbers are descriptive, but they display the full plurality-vote signature of Section~\ref{sec:condorcet}: aggregation pays where the mode is right and is destructive where it is wrong.

Within the high-dissent region, disagreement alone does not guarantee gains, and the exception is instructive. The highest-dissent cells in the study are LogiQA under GPT-6-Luna and Gemini3.8-Flash. GPT-6-Luna shows the full regime structure in one cell: greedy answers 60\% correctly, the six-sample majority drops to 40\% because the modal sampled answer is wrong, while the paraphrase majority rises to 80\% and takes the combined ensemble with it. Gemini3.8-Flash's LogiQA cell ($n=3$) shows the same signature at smaller scale. The plurality-vote model of Section~\ref{sec:condorcet} summarizes the pattern: disagreement is a necessary condition for aggregation to matter, but the sign of the gain is determined by whether the modal answer is correct, which the share does not measure.

The budget curves in Figure~\ref{fig:budget} complete the picture, with the single-sample baseline S1 at $k=1$ and the greedy level marked as a dotted line; panel headers state how many percentage points one question contributes, since a single flipped question moves accuracy by 1.1 to 3.0 points at these sample sizes. For the flagships the curves are flat at the greedy level within one question at every budget, with the one-question deviations reflecting individual tie-breaks rather than trends: Qwen3.8-Max dips one question under PE at $k \in \{2,4\}$, and Qwen3.8-Omni wiggles one question above greedy under SC-4. For Agnes3.0-Flash, the paraphrase ensemble reaches its full 90.9\% already at $k=2$ while the sample majority needs $k=4$ to catch up, the two curves crossing rather than one leading throughout. For Agnes2.5-Flash the ordering is the reverse and persists: sample voting climbs to 95.0\% by $k=6$ while paraphrase voting plateaus at 92.5\%. The practical reading is that the choice between the two sources matters less than the choice of whether to aggregate at all, which the disagreement regime determines, and that neither source dominates the other in budget profile across models.

\subsection{Isolating the prompt-space effect: greedy paraphrases}
\label{sec:greedypara}

The standard paraphrase ensemble decodes each paraphrase once at $\tau = 0.7$, so its diversity mixes prompt-space variation with decoding stochasticity. The greedy-paraphrase control removes the second component by decoding each paraphrase at $\tau = 0$, yielding the noise-free ensemble $\mathrm{PE}^{g}\text{-}6$ and a fragility indicator in which neither side involves sampling. Table~\ref{tab:greedypara} reports the control for the five models whose provider quota allowed it.

The control separates three effects. First, for the flagship, degeneracy is not a temperature artifact: 98.9\% of Qwen3.8-Max's questions return six identical answers even under greedy decoding of six different paraphrases, and the greedy paraphrase ensemble returns the same answer as the sampled paraphrase ensemble on 88 of 88 questions. The collapse is a property of the model-task pair, not of the sampling distribution around it. Second, for the mid-tier models, the prompt-space effect carries the paraphrase ensemble almost entirely: the PE$^{g}$-6 and PE-6 modal answers coincide on 31 of 32 questions for Agnes3.0-Flash and 38 of 39 for Agnes2.5-Flash, so the stochastic component of PE contributes almost nothing beyond the paraphrases themselves in these cells. Third, for the prompt-brittle models, decoding noise and prompt variation interact: GPT-6-Luna's greedy paraphrase ensemble reaches 80.0\% against 86.7\% for the sampled version with modal-answer agreement on only 11 of 15 questions, and Gemini3.8-Flash 83.3\% against 91.7\% with agreement on 10 of 12, so for the most fragile models the sampled paraphrase ensemble retains a two-to-eight point edge over its noise-free counterpart, and the two ensembles disagree on a quarter of the questions. Fragility itself survives the control in strengthened form: GPT-6-Luna flips on 40.0\% of questions and Gemini3.8-Flash on 25.0\% when both sides are decoded greedily, which rules out sampling noise as the cause of paraphrase-induced flips and locates the instability in the model's response to the prompt's surface form.

Chain-of-thought answering raises accuracy where it completes, but it does not dissolve the regime structure. For the flagship subset, CoT lifts Qwen3.8-Max from 88.9\% to 100\% on its nine completed questions while leaving the answer distribution fully degenerate: entropy stays at zero and all six samples agree on every question, so self-consistency remains vacuous. The pattern that survives the mode change is the important one: aggregation gains track the entropy regime in both modes, the degenerate stratum stays degenerate under CoT for the flagship, and the paraphrase side continues to carry signal only where entropy exists. Absolute accuracies are not comparable across modes, the CoT subsets are small, and the slow long-form generations bounded coverage for the rate-limited models, so we read this condition as a robustness check on the regime structure rather than as a mode comparison.

\subsection{Budget efficiency: early stopping and adaptive allocation}
\label{sec:efficiency}

The regime structure implies that most of a fixed budget is spent confirming answers that additional calls cannot change. Two simulated policies quantify the waste; both are replays of our own data rather than new methods, and both descend from existing work on sample-adaptive stopping: the sequential unanimity rule is a special case of the early-stopping self-consistency line \citep{aggarwal2023adaptive,li2024escape}, and we read our simulation as a reproduction of that line's finding in the direct-answering, near-degenerate regime rather than as a novel contribution. The unanimity rule draws canonical samples until the first $k$ at which all drawn samples agree, answers with that unanimous vote, and otherwise falls back to the six-sample majority. The adaptive allocation rule of Section~\ref{sec:discussion} probes each question with two canonical samples and two paraphrases, then routes the remaining budget to paraphrases, resampling, or a split according to which side shows diversity. Table~\ref{tab:efficiency} reports both policies against the fixed conditions, with the probe's calls charged to the adaptive rule; its mean budget runs 4.1 to 4.7 calls, 68 to 78 percent of the six-call budget.

The unanimity rule preserves SC-6 accuracy within one question for every model at a mean budget of 2.0 to 2.7 calls, because degenerate questions announce themselves after two or three unanimous draws. The adaptive rule matches the best fixed six-call strategy on four of the six models and trails it by exactly one question on the remaining two, Qwen3.8-Omni (91.5 against 93.0) and Agnes2.5-Flash (92.5 against 95.0), while spending a mean of 4.1 to 4.7 calls, 68 to 78 percent of the six-call budget; it never exceeds the best fixed strategy on any model, and the one-question deficits are exactly the questions whose diversity the two-call probe did not reveal. Because the rule was designed and evaluated on the same collected matrices, this simulation is a consistency check on the rule, not a validation of it; confirming its transfer requires fresh questions, which our free-tier quotas did not allow (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}). The savings are conservative estimates: the simulation commits to a fixed routing rule after the probe rather than continuing to monitor agreement as later calls arrive, and it charges the full probe even on questions where one call would have sufficed. Both simulations are exact replays of the released call matrices, so they can be re-derived from the logs.
