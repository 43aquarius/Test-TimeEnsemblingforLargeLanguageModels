\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented a budget-matched comparison of the two diversity sources available to test-time ensembling for large language models: resampling the decoder on a fixed prompt, and perturbing the prompt across validated semantic paraphrases. A sixteen-call matrix per question per model, evaluated on six models from four families and six benchmarks, allowed every condition, including self-consistency, paraphrase ensembling, a hybrid allocation, and a combined ensemble, to be derived from matched calls on identical questions, and follow-up controls isolated the prompt-space contribution with greedy paraphrase decodes, probed harder tasks on MMLU-Pro, and varied the answering mode with chain-of-thought instructions. The results organize into a regime structure. Where models are confident, answer distributions collapse, all aggregation strategies coincide with both single-call baselines, and consistency-based uncertainty estimation loses its signal. Where the votes disagree, the single-sample expectation S1, not greedy decoding, is the correct reference: pooled, self-consistency sits $+0.9$ points above S1, the paraphrase ensemble $0.9$ points below, and the twelve-vote ensemble exactly at S1, so no systematic gain for either diversity source is detected in this data at matched budget, and no per-model test detects a difference between them, though the study is not powered to establish equivalence. What differs is the sign of the gain where the modal answer is wrong: on the two least-covered models, whose logical-reasoning subsets carry a wrong sample-side mode, the paraphrase ensemble sat up to ten points above S1 while resampling sat up to three below --- exploratory at those sample sizes, but the direction the plurality-vote model predicts. Greedy decoding itself proved to be a biased reference that can sit four to eleven points from the sampled expectation on commercial endpoints, through mode locking and truncation artifacts, so we recommend reporting the single-sample expectation alongside every greedy score. An adaptive allocation rule, simulated on the collected matrices at 68 to 78 percent of the six-call budget, matched the best fixed strategy on four of six models, trailed it by one question on the other two, and reproduced the fixed paraphrase ensemble's accuracy on five of six, so its measured value on this data is the call saving rather than accuracy; a sequential unanimity rule, a special case of early-stopping self-consistency, preserved accuracy within one question at two to three calls. A blind replication on 119 fresh questions drawn from the same pools, fully covered by four models from two providers and two families, shows that this null is distribution-specific rather than universal: pooled, the paraphrase ensemble gains $+4.9$ points over S1 (interval $+2.3$ to $+7.6$) and self-consistency $+2.4$ ($+1.1$ to $+3.7$), both with confidence intervals excluding zero, while the orderings between the ensembles remain statistically inseparable from each other; the prompt-space margin concentrates where answer-space diversity is exhausted (SAT-Math pooled: 76.2 for self-consistency against 91.2 for the paraphrase ensemble; per-model gains of 7.8 points for the pure paraphrase vote on one model and 6.0 for the combined ensemble on another, both with intervals excluding zero) and reverses where paraphrases shift the verbal task (LogiQA on the first model, PIQA on the pro-tier DeepSeek). The answer to the title question is therefore conditional: prompt-space ensembling pays when resampling cannot diversify and paraphrases preserve task semantics; sample-space ensembling pays when paraphrases distort the task; and in the degenerate regime that dominates current benchmarks, neither pays and the budget is better saved. We release prompts, paraphrases, and raw predictions to support replication as model behavior continues to shift.
