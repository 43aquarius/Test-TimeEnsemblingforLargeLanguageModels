\section{A Budget-Matched Framework for Test-Time Ensembling}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Setup and notation}

Let $M$ denote a large language model and let $q$ denote a question with a gold answer $y^*(q)$. For multiple-choice tasks the answer is an option letter, and for free-form numeric tasks the answer is a value compared numerically. A prompt $x$ is a rendering of $q$ into natural language, consisting of an optional passage, a question stem, an option list, and an instruction that specifies the required output format. We distinguish one canonical prompt $x_0$ and $K$ paraphrased prompts $x_1, \dots, x_K$ that preserve the semantics of $x_0$ under a validation procedure described in Section~\ref{sec:setup}.

The model produces an answer through stochastic decoding. We write $a(M, x, s)$ for the answer extracted from the $s$-th independent decode of prompt $x$ at temperature $\tau = 0.7$, and $a_g(M, x)$ for the greedy decode at $\tau = 0$. Answers are extracted from the model output by a fixed parser that searches for the mandated answer format and falls back to pattern matching over the final output lines; the parse rate is reported in Section~\ref{sec:setup}, together with a retry policy and a sensitivity analysis for failed extractions.

\subsection{The call matrix}

For every question and model we collect a matrix of sixteen calls:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{C}(M, q) \;=\; \bigl\{ a_g(M, x_0) \bigr\} \;\cup\; \bigcup_{s=1}^{S} \bigl\{ a(M, x_0, s) \bigr\} \;\cup\; \bigcup_{k=1}^{K} \bigl\{ a(M, x_k, s) : s \le r_k \bigr\},
\label{eq:matrix}
\end{equation}
with $S = 6$ samples on the canonical prompt, $K = 6$ paraphrases, and $r_k = 2$ decodes for the first three paraphrases and $r_k = 1$ for the remaining three. The matrix therefore contains one greedy call, six canonical samples, six first-decode paraphrase calls, and three second-decode paraphrase calls. Every experimental condition studied in this paper is a deterministic function of this matrix, which guarantees that all comparisons hold the question, the model, and the realized decoding randomness constant across conditions.

\subsection{Ensemble conditions}

All conditions aggregate votes by majority rule. Ties are broken in favor of the earliest vote in a fixed canonical order, which prefers the canonical prompt over paraphrases and earlier paraphrases over later ones; we report tie incidence, which is negligible except where noted.

The \emph{self-consistency} condition of size $k$ aggregates the first $k$ canonical samples:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{SC}\text{-}k(q) \;=\; \operatorname*{majority}\bigl( a(M, x_0, 1), \dots, a(M, x_0, k) \bigr), \qquad k \le 6 .
\end{equation}
The \emph{paraphrase ensemble} of size $k$ aggregates one decode of each of the first $k$ paraphrases:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{PE}\text{-}k(q) \;=\; \operatorname*{majority}\bigl( a(M, x_1, 1), \dots, a(M, x_k, 1) \bigr), \qquad k \le 6 .
\end{equation}
Both conditions spend exactly $k$ model calls and differ only in where the diversity comes from: $\mathrm{SC}\text{-}k$ perturbs the decoder while the prompt is fixed, whereas $\mathrm{PE}\text{-}k$ perturbs the prompt while the decoder configuration is fixed. This is the budget match that makes the two conditions comparable.

The \emph{hybrid} condition splits a budget of six calls between the two sources, using two decodes of each of the first three paraphrases:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{HB}\text{-}6(q) \;=\; \operatorname*{majority}\bigl( a(M, x_k, s) : k \in \{1,2,3\},\ s \in \{1,2\} \bigr).
\end{equation}
Finally, the \emph{combined} condition aggregates all twelve stochastic votes available in the matrix, $\mathrm{COMB}\text{-}12(q) = \operatorname{majority}\bigl( \mathrm{SC}\text{-}6(q) \cup \mathrm{PE}\text{-}6(q) \bigr)$, and serves as a twelve-call reference point for the marginal value of doubling the budget. Three further baselines and controls complete the design. The \emph{single-sample} baseline $\mathrm{S1}$ is one stochastic draw of the canonical prompt; its expected accuracy is estimated by averaging over the six canonical samples, and it differs from the greedy baseline whenever temperature-$0.7$ decoding and temperature-$0$ decoding disagree. A \emph{greedy-paraphrase} control re-decodes each paraphrase at $\tau = 0$, giving $\mathrm{PE}^{g}\text{-}6(q) = \operatorname{majority}\bigl( a_g(M, x_1), \dots, a_g(M, x_6) \bigr)$; comparing $\mathrm{PE}^{g}\text{-}6$ with $\mathrm{PE}\text{-}6$ isolates the contribution of prompt-space variation from the decoding stochasticity that the standard paraphrase ensemble mixes in. A chain-of-thought variant repeats the single-call baseline, the self-consistency condition, and (for stem-locus datasets) the paraphrase ensemble under an explicit reasoning instruction, and a hard-task probe repeats the single-call and self-consistency conditions on MMLU-Pro; both are specified in Section~\ref{sec:setup} and analyzed in Section~\ref{sec:results}. The greedy decode $a_g(M, x_0)$ serves as the single-call baseline.

\subsection{Consistency, entropy, and fragility}

Three per-question statistics summarize the answer distribution. \emph{Sample agreement} is the frequency of the modal answer among the six canonical samples,
\begin{equation}
c_{\mathrm{S}}(q) \;=\; \max_{v} \frac{1}{6} \sum_{s=1}^{6} \mathbb{1}\bigl[ a(M, x_0, s) = v \bigr],
\end{equation}
and \emph{paraphrase agreement} $c_{\mathrm{P}}(q)$ is defined analogously over the six first-decode paraphrase answers. Both lie in $(0, 1]$, and the value $1$ indicates that the corresponding source produced no diversity at all, a situation we call \emph{answer degeneracy}. The \emph{support-normalized answer entropy} is the Shannon entropy of the empirical answer distribution over the twelve stochastic votes, divided by the maximum entropy attainable with the observed number of distinct answers; it equals one when the observed answers are uniformly distributed over their support and zero when they coincide. We use this normalization throughout, and all entropy values reported in the paper use it.

\emph{Fragility} captures disagreement between the two views of the same question: a question is fragile for a model when the majority answer of the canonical samples differs from the majority answer of the paraphrase answers, that is, when $\mathrm{SC}\text{-}6(q) \ne \mathrm{PE}\text{-}6(q)$. Fragility measures how much of the model's answer is an artifact of the particular surface form of the prompt rather than of the underlying question. The greedy-paraphrase control supports a noise-free variant, $\mathrm{SC}\text{-}6(q) \ne \mathrm{PE}^{g}\text{-}6(q)$, in which neither side involves stochastic decoding.

\subsection{Error detection and selective prediction}

We evaluate consistency scores as unsupervised error detectors. For each model, the consistency score of a question (reversed, so that lower agreement indicates higher predicted risk) ranks questions, and we compute the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) for predicting whether the reference answer is incorrect, where the reference is the combined twelve-vote ensemble unless stated otherwise. When both agreement scores are available, the \emph{combined} detector is their sum of disagreements, $2 - c_{\mathrm{S}} - c_{\mathrm{P}}$, so that a question is flagged when either source shows diversity; this is the synthesis used in the Both columns of Section~\ref{sec:results}. We complement AUROC with risk-coverage curves that plot the error rate of the accepted subset as the acceptance threshold varies, summarized by the area under the risk-coverage curve (AURC), computed by accepting questions in decreasing order of confidence and averaging the error rate over all prefix sizes; smaller AURC indicates a better ranking, and a random ranking yields AURC equal to the overall error rate. The oracle-excess AURC (E-AURC) measures the gap to the best achievable ranking \citep{guo2017calibration,lin2024uncertainty}. Where a model produces zero errors or constant scores, AUROC is undefined and we report the regime instead; where a model produces fewer than five errors, we report AUROC and AURC descriptively only, without confidence intervals or cross-model comparison.

Because the agreement scores and the reference answer share the same underlying votes, part of the error-detection result is structural rather than empirical: when all twelve votes coincide, the reference equals that unanimous answer by construction, and any detector that reads unanimity is correct by definition on such questions. We therefore separate the degenerate stratum, where the detector outcome is definitional, from the non-degenerate stratum, where the ranking carries empirical content, and we report both. As an additional check against circularity, we recompute the entropy-gain correlation using disjoint votes: entropy over the canonical samples is correlated with the paraphrase-ensemble gain, and entropy over the paraphrase votes with the self-consistency gain, so that the predictor and the outcome share no votes.

\subsection{Statistical analysis}

Accuracy comparisons between two conditions on the same questions are paired by construction. We report bootstrap 95\% confidence intervals for accuracy differences (10,000 resamples, resampling questions) and two-sided exact McNemar tests for paired correctness indicators. Because we interpret a difference of three percentage points or less as practically negligible, we also report two one-sided tests (TOST) against equivalence bounds of $\pm 3$ points, implemented as bootstrap 90\% confidence intervals for the paired difference: equivalence is established only when the entire interval lies inside the bounds. A failure to reject the null hypothesis of no difference is reported as \emph{no detectable difference} and never as evidence of equivalence; at our sample sizes, differences smaller than roughly five points often remain undetectable, and we state this power limitation explicitly wherever it applies. Pooled analyses aggregate (model, question) pairs that share questions across models; because such pairs are not independent, all pooled confidence intervals use a question-level cluster bootstrap that resamples questions and carries every model's pair within a resampled question. Per-model analyses use each model's own completed question set, with the sample size stated in every table. We treat question sets as fixed and do not extrapolate beyond the measured benchmarks.

\subsection{When does majority voting help? A simple model}
\label{sec:condorcet}

The empirical regime structure has a counterpart in a simple plurality-vote model that generalizes the Condorcet jury theorem to the case where votes are independent and identically distributed over a fixed answer set \citep{condorcet1785essai}. Write $p$ for the probability that one vote is the correct answer and $q_1 \ge q_2 \ge \dots$ for the probabilities of the incorrect answers, so that $p + \sum_i q_i = 1$.

\textbf{Proposition 1.} If $p > q_1$, the probability that the plurality winner of $k$ independent votes is the correct answer increases monotonically toward one as $k$ grows. If $q_1 > p$, this probability decreases monotonically toward zero.

\textbf{Proposition 2.} Under greedy voting ($k = 1$), the success probability is $p$. Hence majority voting helps if and only if $p > q_1$, and it hurts exactly when the model's most likely answer is wrong.

The proofs follow the standard Condorcet argument: the plurality winner coincides with the maximum-likelihood estimate of the modal answer, so the winner converges in probability to the mode of the vote distribution as $k$ grows, which is the correct answer precisely when $p > q_1$; the $k = 1$ case is immediate. The propositions explain the three regimes we observe. In the degenerate regime, $p$ is zero or one and voting is vacuous. In the high-entropy regime with $p > q_1$ (for example Agnes3.0-Flash on logical reasoning), self-consistency converts a distribution that is right on average into a reliable majority. In the high-entropy regime with $q_1 > p$ (GPT-6-Luna on logical reasoning), voting amplifies the modal error and reduces accuracy, while a second diversity source that shifts the effective vote distribution can restore $p > q_1$. The model also clarifies why answer entropy alone does not predict gains: entropy measures the spread of the distribution, whereas the sign of $p - q_1$ measures whether the mode is correct, and two distributions with identical entropy can sit on opposite sides of that threshold. The budget-matched comparison of Section~\ref{sec:results} can then be read as asking which diversity source moves the effective vote distribution further toward the $p > q_1$ region at a given budget.
