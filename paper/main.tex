\title{When Does Test-Time Ensembling Help Large Language Models? A Budget-Matched Study of Answer-Space and Prompt-Space Diversity}

\author[inst1]{Chang Tan\corref{cor1}}
\ead{aquars43@foxmail.com}
\cortext[cor1]{Corresponding author.}
\address[inst1]{Dalian University of Technology, Dalian, Liaoning, China}

\begin{abstract}
Test-time ensembling improves the reliability of large language models by aggregating answers from multiple model calls instead of trusting a single greedy prediction. Two families of strategies dominate practice. Self-consistency resamples the stochastic decoder on one fixed prompt, so the ensemble varies along what we call the answer space. Prompt ensembling instead fixes the decoding configuration and varies the prompt over semantically equivalent paraphrases, so the ensemble varies along the prompt space. Both families consume the same scarce resource, namely the number of model calls, yet prior work studies them in isolation and the question of which source of diversity buys more accuracy per call remains open. We present a controlled, budget-matched comparison across six commercial large language models and six reasoning and knowledge benchmarks under a unified protocol in which every question is answered sixteen times per model: six decodes of the original prompt, six paraphrased prompts answered once each, a hybrid allocation that combines three paraphrases with two decodes each, a twelve-vote combined condition, and a greedy baseline, together with a single-sample baseline, a greedy-paraphrase control that isolates prompt-space effects from decoding stochasticity, a chain-of-thought variant, and a hard-task probe on MMLU-Pro. The results reveal a regime structure that current evaluation practice overlooks. On benchmarks that modern flagship models saturate, the sampled answer distribution degenerates to a single value, both ensembles collapse onto the greedy baseline, and consistency-based uncertainty estimates lose their discriminative power, which raises a caution for sampling-based confidence in this regime. Where genuine answer entropy exists, both ensembles lift accuracy over greedy decoding, no significant difference between the two diversity sources is detected at matched budgets (though the study is not powered to establish equivalence), a single stochastic draw can differ from greedy decoding by ten points, majority voting can even reduce accuracy when the modal answer is wrong, and the two agreement signals flag partially disjoint errors across models. We distill the findings into an adaptive allocation rule that matches or exceeds every fixed strategy in simulation at two-thirds of the budget, and we release all prompts, paraphrases, and raw predictions to support reproducible evaluation.
\end{abstract}
