\title{When Does Test-Time Ensembling Help Large Language Models? A Budget-Matched Study of Answer-Space and Prompt-Space Diversity}

\author[inst1]{Chang Tan\corref{cor1}}
\ead{aquars43@foxmail.com}
\cortext[cor1]{Corresponding author.}
\address[inst1]{Dalian University of Technology, Dalian, Liaoning, China}

\begin{abstract}
Test-time ensembling improves the reliability of large language models by aggregating answers from multiple model calls instead of trusting a single prediction. Two families of strategies dominate practice. Self-consistency resamples the stochastic decoder on one fixed prompt, so the ensemble varies along what we call the answer space. Prompt ensembling instead fixes the decoding configuration and varies the prompt over semantically equivalent paraphrases, so the ensemble varies along the prompt space. Both families consume the same scarce resource, namely the number of model calls, yet prior work studies them in isolation and the question of which source of diversity buys more accuracy per call remains open. We present a controlled, budget-matched comparison across six commercial large language models and six reasoning and knowledge benchmarks under a unified protocol in which every question is answered sixteen times per model: six decodes of the original prompt, six paraphrased prompts answered once each, a hybrid allocation that combines three paraphrases with two decodes each, a twelve-vote combined condition, and a greedy baseline, together with a single-sample baseline S1 estimated as the expected accuracy of one stochastic draw, a greedy-paraphrase control that isolates prompt-space effects from decoding stochasticity, a chain-of-thought variant, and a hard-task probe on MMLU-Pro. The results reveal a regime structure that current evaluation practice overlooks, and they turn on the choice of the $k{=}1$ reference. On benchmarks that modern flagship models saturate, the sampled answer distribution degenerates to a single value, both ensembles collapse onto both single-call baselines, and consistency-based uncertainty estimates lose their discriminative power, which raises a caution for sampling-based confidence in this regime. Where the votes disagree, measured against S1, no systematic gain for either ensemble is detected in this data: pooled over the four well-covered models, six-vote self-consistency sits $+0.9$ points above S1 and the six-paraphrase ensemble $0.9$ points below, with the twelve-vote ensemble exactly at S1 and 104 of the 112 pooled model-question pairs unanimous, and no per-model test detects a difference between the two sources, though the study is not powered to establish equivalence. The differences that do appear are exploratory and track whether the modal answer is correct: on the two least-covered models, where the sample-side modal answer is wrong on the logical-reasoning subsets, the paraphrase ensemble gains $5.6$ to $10.0$ points over S1 with intervals touching zero, while self-consistency loses $2.8$ to $3.3$ with intervals spanning zero. A pre-drawn fresh-question expansion set (119 unseen questions, three models fully covered across two providers) upgrades this exploratory picture out of sample: pooled, self-consistency gains $+2.3$ points over S1 and the paraphrase ensemble $+5.1$, both intervals excluding zero, yet the ensembles remain statistically inseparable from each other ($2.8$ points apart, interval touching zero); the prompt-space margin concentrates where answer-space diversity is exhausted, on computation-deterministic datasets and in the weak-majority stratum, while restating verbal-reasoning stems can reverse it, and on one model the hybrid and combined allocations, not the pure ensembles, carry the significant gain. Greedy decoding proves to be a biased reference, sitting four to eleven points below the sampled expectation on two models through mode locking and truncation artifacts, so apparent gains measured against greedy largely reflect the reference, not the ensembling; on the affected endpoints, switching the greedy call to a single stochastic draw recovers the gap at no extra cost. An adaptive allocation rule simulated on the collected matrices reproduces the fixed paraphrase ensemble on five of six models at 68 to 78 percent of its calls and matches the best fixed strategy on four of six, trailing it by one question on the rest, so its measured value is the call saving; on the fresh set it transfers in budget (79 to 87 percent of calls) but not in allocation on two of the three fully covered models, trailing the best fixed strategy by up to two questions, while on the third it strictly dominates every fixed allocation; and a sequential unanimity rule, a special case of early-stopping self-consistency, preserves accuracy within one question at two to three calls. We release all prompts, paraphrases, and raw predictions to support reproducible evaluation.
\end{abstract}
